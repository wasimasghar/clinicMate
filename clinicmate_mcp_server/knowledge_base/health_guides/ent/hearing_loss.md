# Hearing Loss

## What is hearing loss
Hearing loss means you cannot hear sounds as well as a person with normal hearing. It can be mild (missing soft sounds and whispers), moderate (difficulty following normal talk), severe or profound (hearing almost nothing). It can affect one ear or both ears, and it can come slowly over years or suddenly within hours or days.

## Types of hearing loss
- Conductive hearing loss: sound cannot pass through the outer or middle ear, for example because of ear wax, fluid behind the ear drum, an ear infection or a hole in the ear drum. It is often treatable.
- Sensorineural hearing loss: the inner ear (cochlea) or the hearing nerve is damaged, for example by age or loud noise. It is usually permanent, but hearing aids or a cochlear implant can help a lot.
- Mixed hearing loss: both types together.

## Why hearing loss happens
- Age: hearing slowly becomes weaker, usually after about 60 years of age. Both ears are affected and high sounds go first.
- Loud noise: headphones or earphones at high volume, loud music at weddings and concerts, factory machines, generators, traffic horns and fireworks. Noise damage builds up over years and does not come back.
- Ear infections, especially repeated infections or a long-standing discharge from the ear.
- Ear wax blocking the ear canal.
- Injury to the head or ear, or putting cotton buds, keys, pins or matchsticks inside the ear.
- Some infections such as meningitis, measles, mumps and rubella (German measles in pregnancy can affect the baby's hearing).
- Some medicines can harm hearing. Never stop or start a medicine without your doctor.
- Problems at birth, low birth weight, severe jaundice in newborns, or hearing loss in the family (genetic).

## Signs of hearing loss in adults
- Turning up the TV or phone volume more than others in the house.
- Asking people to repeat themselves, or saying "kya?" often.
- Difficulty hearing in noisy places like weddings, markets or restaurants.
- Hearing people speak but not understanding the words clearly.
- Ringing, buzzing or whistling sound in the ear (tinnitus).
- Feeling tired or stressed after conversations, or avoiding gatherings.

## Signs of hearing loss in children
- A baby does not get startled by loud sounds or does not turn towards voices by about 6 months.
- Speech starts late, or the child speaks less clearly than other children of the same age.
- The child does not respond when called, watches TV very close or very loud.
- Poor attention or falling marks at school.
Early testing is very important because hearing is needed to learn speech.

## Hearing tests
- Audiometry: you listen to beeps through headphones and press a button when you hear them. It shows how much hearing is lost at each pitch, for each ear.
- Tympanometry: checks how the ear drum moves and whether there is fluid in the middle ear.
- BERA (also called ABR): checks hearing in babies and young children while they sleep, without needing them to respond.
- Newborn hearing screening: a quick and painless test soon after birth.
An Audiologist does hearing tests and fits hearing aids. An ENT Specialist examines the ear for infection, wax, ear drum damage and other medical causes, and decides if surgery is needed.

## Hearing aids
Hearing aids are small electronic devices that make sounds louder and clearer. Common types:
- Behind-the-ear (BTE): sits behind the ear with a thin tube or wire into the ear. Strong and suitable for most levels of hearing loss, including children.
- Receiver-in-canal (RIC): like BTE but smaller, with the speaker inside the ear canal.
- In-the-ear (ITE): custom made to fill the outer part of the ear.
- In-the-canal (ITC) and completely-in-canal (CIC): very small and less visible, for mild to moderate loss.
- Rechargeable hearing aids: charged at night like a phone, no small batteries to change.
- Bluetooth hearing aids: connect to mobile phones and TV so calls and sound go straight into the ears.
The right type depends on the hearing test, the shape of the ear, the person's age and budget. An Audiologist chooses, programs and adjusts the hearing aid.

## Getting used to a hearing aid
- The brain needs a few weeks to get used to new sounds. Start with a few hours a day in quiet places and increase slowly.
- Your own voice and background noise may sound strange at first. This usually settles.
- Go back to the Audiologist for adjustment if sounds are too loud, too soft or whistling.
- Wearing hearing aids in both ears (when both ears are affected) usually gives better and more natural hearing.

## Caring for hearing aids
- Keep them dry: remove before bathing, swimming or wudu, and store them in a dry box at night.
- Clean wax from the tip daily with a soft brush or cloth.
- Keep away from heat, children and pets.
- Change or charge batteries regularly and keep spare batteries.
- Get them checked by the Audiologist at least once a year.

## Cochlear implant
A cochlear implant is a small electronic device placed by surgery inside the inner ear. It is for people with severe or profound sensorineural hearing loss who get little benefit from hearing aids. It sends sound signals directly to the hearing nerve. Children born deaf benefit most when implanted early, often before 2 to 3 years of age. After surgery, the implant is switched on and tuned (mapping) by an Audiologist, and speech therapy helps the person learn to use the new hearing.

## Preventing hearing loss
- Follow the 60/60 rule for earphones: not more than 60% volume for not more than 60 minutes at a time, then give the ears a break.
- Prefer over-ear headphones with noise cancelling, so you do not need to turn the volume up in noisy places.
- Use ear plugs or ear muffs in factories, workshops and near generators.
- Stand away from loudspeakers at weddings and events.
- Never put cotton buds, keys, pins or oil inside the ear. Ear wax usually comes out by itself.
- Get children vaccinated on time (measles, mumps, rubella and meningitis protection).
- Get ear infections treated early, especially in children.
- Get a hearing test if you are over 60, work in noise, or notice any signs.

## When to see a doctor
- Hearing loss, ringing or a blocked feeling that lasts more than a few days.
- Pain, discharge or bad smell from the ear.
- A child with late speech or not responding to sounds.
- Hearing loss in only one ear.

## Emergency: sudden hearing loss
Sudden hearing loss in one or both ears, within hours or up to 3 days, is an emergency. See an ENT Specialist the same day if possible. Early treatment, ideally within 72 hours, gives the best chance of getting hearing back. Also go to emergency at once if hearing loss comes with severe headache, weakness of the face or body, slurred speech or severe dizziness, as these can be signs of a stroke (call 1122).

## Common questions
- Kam sunai deta hai, kis doctor ke paas jaun? Pehle ENT Specialist, phir hearing test aur hearing aid ke liye Audiologist.
- Hearing aid kitni qisam ke hote hain? Behind-the-ear, in-the-ear, chhote in-the-canal, rechargeable aur Bluetooth wale.
- Headphones se kaan kharab hote hain? Tez awaaz par lambi der sunne se haan. 60/60 rule follow karein.
- Kaan mein seeti ki awaaz aati hai? Isay tinnitus kehte hain, ENT ya Audiologist ko dikhayein.
- Achanak sunna band ho gaya? Ye emergency hai, usi din ENT doctor ko dikhayein.
- Bachcha awaaz par react nahi karta? Jaldi hearing test (BERA) karwayein.
- Kaan mein tel daal sakte hain? Nahi, kaan mein tel ya cotton bud na daalein.
